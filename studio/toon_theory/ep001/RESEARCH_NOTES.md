# EP001 RESEARCH NOTES (verified)

All DOIs resolved through Crossref (metadata match). Findings below are taken from the published abstracts (Crossref, Europe PMC, OpenAlex) unless stated.

## 1. Uncertainty and attraction: Whitchurch, Wilson & Gilbert (2011)
DOI 10.1177/0956797610393745, *Psychological Science* 22(2):172-175.
- **Finding (abstract):** College women viewed Facebook profiles of four male students and were told the men liked them a lot, an average amount, or either. The uncertain group was most attracted, even more than the "likes you a lot" group, and reported thinking about the men most; that thinking increased attraction.
- **Limits:** one small study of college women (about 47 per secondary reports); hypothetical profiles; the "attraction" measure has been criticised as mostly behavioural intentions; one unpublished thesis-level replication attempt found no effect. **Script wording:** "small, college students. Don't quote me."

## 2. Investment and commitment: Le & Agnew (2003); Rusbult (1980)
- Le & Agnew, DOI 10.1111/1475-6811.00035, *Personal Relationships* 10(1):37-57. **Meta-analysis (abstract):** 52 studies, 60 samples, 11,582 participants; satisfaction, alternatives and investments each correlated with commitment and together explained nearly two-thirds of its variance; commitment predicted breakup.
- Rusbult, DOI 10.1016/0022-1031(80)90007-4, *JESP* 16(2):172-186. **Abstract:** two experiments (role-play and a survey of ongoing relationships); commitment rose with investment size and fell with the value of alternatives.
- **Limits:** correlational, largely self-report, student/convenience samples in the early work. **Script wording:** "Sunk cost with feelings" is a character's paraphrase; it does not say people cannot leave.

## 3. Idealisation: Murray, Holmes & Griffin (1996) **[condition 1: now verified from the published abstract]**
- *JPSP* 70(1):79-98, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.70.1.79. **Abstract (via OpenAlex):** satisfaction is associated with idealised rather than realistic perceptions of one's partner; individuals were happier when they idealised their partners and their partners idealised them; "a certain degree of idealization or illusion may be a critical feature of satisfying dating and even marital relationships."
- Companion paper, *JPSP* 71(6):1155-1180, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.71.6.1155: over a year, dating couples who idealised each other most were most likely to stay together and reported greater increases in satisfaction and decreases in conflict.
- **Limits:** self-report and path analyses (correlational/longitudinal, not experimental); mutual idealisation in couples. The papers do **not** test what happens when one partner never "rises" to the picture; Ankush/Krish's "drawing a nice picture of someone who isn't there" is **dramatic interpretation, not a finding**. **Script wording** keeps "Apparently".
