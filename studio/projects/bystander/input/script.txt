A person collapses in a crowded subway station. Dozens of people see it. So why might nobody move? Everyone looks around, and everyone sees the same thing. Nobody else is panicking. So it must be fine. Someone else will help. Someone else has probably called already. Psychologists call this the bystander effect. In 1968, John Darley and Bibb Latané ran an experiment. A student heard someone having a seizure over an intercom. When people thought they were alone, most of them ran to help. When they thought four others could hear it too, far fewer did, and they were slower. The responsibility gets split between everyone watching, until each person feels almost none of it. But here's the twist. When researchers studied real CCTV footage of public fights, someone stepped in about nine times out of ten. Crowds often do help. The danger is those frozen first few seconds. So if you ever need help, don't shout at the crowd. Point at one person. You, in the red jacket. Call an ambulance. Would you have been the first to move? Tell me in the comments.
