Imagine losing your hand. And then, every night, feeling it. Your fingers clench. It itches. It hurts. But there's nothing there. This is phantom limb, and most people who lose a limb experience it. Here's why. Your brain keeps a map of your body, a strip of tissue where every part of you has its own address. When the hand is gone, the map doesn't get the memo. The hand's address is still there, still firing. Even stranger, the face sits right next to the hand on that map. So in some patients, touching their cheek, makes them feel their missing fingers. In the nineteen nineties, neuroscientist V. S. Ramachandran tried something simple. A box, with a mirror. Patients watched the reflection of their good hand, and for some of them, the pain in the phantom began to ease. Now flip it around. In the rubber hand experiment, people watched a fake hand being stroked, while their real hand was hidden. Within minutes, many felt the rubber hand was theirs. So where does your body actually end? Maybe not at your skin. Maybe wherever your brain decides. Have you ever felt something that wasn't there? Tell me in the comments.
