You give a stranger one hundred dollars. Forty-five days later, he hands you back one hundred and fifty. Exactly on time.
That's the scam.
Boston, 1920. Charles Ponzi is broke, until he finds a loophole: a tiny postal coupon you can buy cheap in Italy and swap for stamps worth more in America.
Buy low. Swap high. Get rich.
So he makes one promise: fifty percent profit in forty-five days.
Here's the crazy part. He pays. Every early investor gets their money. On time.
They tell their friends. Their friends empty their savings. Some mortgage their homes.
Soon he's taking in a million dollars a week. Cash in closets. Cash in drawers. Cash in wastebaskets.
He buys a mansion. Then, because why not, he buys a bank.
But nobody asks the obvious question: if everyone's getting paid… where's the money coming from?
One reporter does the math. To pay everyone, Ponzi would need one hundred and sixty million coupons.
There are twenty-seven thousand. In the whole world.
There was never a loophole. Your one hundred and fifty dollars? Just the next guy's one hundred.
The headlines hit. The crowds turn. Six banks collapse.
Forty thousand people lose twenty million dollars. Ponzi dies broke, with seventy-five dollars to his name.
But his trick never died. Crypto apps. WhatsApp groups. Anything that says "guaranteed."
So if a stranger ever hands you back one hundred and fifty…
ask whose hundred it really was.
