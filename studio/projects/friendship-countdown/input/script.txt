Your best friend's number says 41.

It's floating right above Kabir's head. Small. White. Glowing. Forty-one.

You blink. It doesn't go away.

You look around the college canteen. Everyone has one. Riya from your class 2,300. The guy who borrowed your charger once 3. Your mom, on a video call no number at all. Just a warm, gold light.

And slowly, you understand. Every number is how many days that friendship has left.

At first, it's a superpower. That fake friend who only texts when he needs notes? Twelve days. You stop helping him and you're right. He disappears.

The girl who sits alone in the library? Her number with you is huge. Eleven thousand days. Thirty years. You say hi. She becomes the funniest person you know.

And here's the strange part. It's real science. Researchers found it takes around fifty hours to turn a stranger into a casual friend and more than two hundred hours to make a close one. Friendship isn't luck. It's time.

So you start spending time like money.

But Kabir's number keeps falling. 41. 30. 22.

You've known him since you were seven. Cricket in the gully. Exam nights. The worst haircut of your life, and he still said it looked fine.

What could possibly end in 22 days?

You try everything. Free food, movie nights, old memes at 2 a.m. The number doesn't move.

And you notice something else. Your numbers with everyone are shrinking. Because you're so busy checking numbers you've stopped actually listening.

A famous Dutch study found people lose about half of their close friends every seven years. Not because of fights. Because of silence. Friendships rarely explode. They fade.

 Then, on day 3, Kabir finally says it.

"I got the job in Canada. I leave on Sunday. I didn't know how to tell you."

That's the number. Not a fight. A flight.

Three days.

You could be angry. You could act like it doesn't matter. Instead, you do the only thing the number never measured.

You show up.

Day 3, you skip class and you play cricket in the old gully, two grown men in the rain, laughing like idiots.

Day 2, you write down every stupid memory you have and put it in his bag.

Day 1, the airport. He hugs you so hard your glasses break.

 And at the gate, his number flickers. 0. Then it changes.

It becomes a symbol you've never seen before. An infinity sign. Glowing gold. The same light as your mom's.

Because some friendships don't end. They just move to a different time zone.

The longest-running study on happiness, at Harvard, followed people for over eighty years. Its biggest finding? The people who lived the longest, happiest lives weren't the richest. They were the ones with good relationships.

You walk out of the airport. You check the mirror in the taxi.

There's a number above your own head.

It's not counting friendships. It's counting how many days you have left to call the people you miss.
